In our project, the feature extraction process follows a clinical-technical pipeline that transforms raw laboratory data into diagnostic signals.

Here is specifically how I extracted features

Direct Biomarker Mapping
I start with a 61-feature panel. These are the raw concentrations of biomarkers (proteins, enzymes, hormones, and genetic indicators) provided in the clinical dataset. Each biomarker is treated as a unique feature dimension.

Statistical Denoising & Cleaning
Before the AI "sees" the features, they undergo a cleaning process in the 

Data Manager

Outlier Removal: I use the IQR (Interquartile Range) method. Any biomarker concentration that falls 1.5x above or below the IQR is removed to ensure the model isn't confused by temporary lab errors.
Normalization: Since different biomarkers have different scales (some in mg/dL, others in μg/L), I apply Min-Max Scaling. This compresses all features into a unified 0 to 1 range, ensuring no single marker dominates the model's decision just because its numerical value is larger.

Target Feature Derivation (Unsupervised Extraction)
One of the most unique extraction steps is using K-Means Clustering to derive the target class.

Because clinical "labels" are often missing in pilot data, the system extracts a "Clinical Risk Category" by identifying natural clusters in the 61-feature space. This turns the raw distribution of biomarkers into a structured binary feature: Healthy (Class 0) vs. Detected (Class 1).

Graph Construction (Structural Extraction)
For the Graph Neural Network (GNN), I perform "Structural Feature Extraction":
The system calculates a Correlation Matrix across the biomarkers.
If two biomarkers have a correlation coefficient $> 0.5$, an edge (connection) is created.
The Result: The AI doesn't just look at the markers as a list; it extracts a "Biological Network" feature where the nodes are biomarkers and the connections represent their physiological interactions.

Topological Embedding (t-SNE)
For the clinician to "see" the features, I use t-SNE (t-Distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding).
This extracts the most important "proximity" features and maps the 61-dimensional biomarker space into a 2D Patient Similarity Map. This allows us to prove that "Detected" patients share a unique biological fingerprint that is topologically distinct from healthy donors.


Summary 
Features are extracted through a multi-stage pipeline: first by cleaning outliers (IQR) and normalizing scales, then by structural correlation mapping (for the GNN), and finally by unsupervised clustering to define the diagnostic risk categories.

We call it: "Diagnostic Data Refinement Pipeline."

These steps ensure that the AI models are analyzing "clean" biological signals rather than mathematical noise.


---

 1. Outlier Removal (The 1.5x IQR Rule)
In clinical diagnostics, an outlier might be a faulty sensor reading or a highly unusual patient case that could confuse the AI.

   How it works: The system calculates the Interquartile Range (IQR)—the distance between the 25th percentile (Q1) and the 75th percentile (Q3). 
   The Filter: Any biomarker value that is more than 1.5 times the IQR below Q1 or above Q3 is considered "clinically extreme" and is removed from the dataset.
   Clinical Goal: To ensure the model learns from typical biological variations rather than extreme "one-off" anomalies.

 2. Normalization (Min-Max Scaling 0 to 1)
Different biomarkers have different units (e.g., pg/ml vs. mg/dl). One biomarker might have values from 0.001 to 0.05, while another ranges from 1,000 to 5,000. 

   How it works: It squashes every value into a fixed range of [0, 1] using the formula:  
    $$\text{Normalized Value} = \frac{(x - \text{Min})}{(\text{Max} - \text{Min})}$$
   Clinical Goal: This "levels the playing field." It prevents biomarkers with naturally larger numbers from appearing "more important" to the AI just because the numbers are bigger.

 3. Standard Scaling (Z-Score Standardization)
Standard Scaling transforms the data such that it has a Mean of 0 and a Standard Deviation of 1.

   How it works: It subtracts the average value and divides by the spread of the data (Standard Deviation) using the formula:  
    $$\text{Standard Score} (z) = \frac{(x - \mu)}{\sigma}$$
   Clinical Goal: This is critical for models like SVM and Logistic Regression. It helps the AI understand how "unusual" a specific biomarker level is relative to the average patient population.

---

 Summary Table for your Report:

| Step | Mathematical Method | Clinical Purpose |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Outlier Cleaning | Interquartile Range (IQR) | Removes "noise" and faulty sensor readings. |
| Normalization | Min-Max Scaling [0, 1] | Brings different biomarker units into a shared range. |
| Standard Scaling | Z-Score (Mean=0, Std=1) | Centers the data for better pattern recognition in AI models. |

Why do we do this?
Without these steps, your AI model might "ignore" a critical biomarker simply because its numerical value is small, or it might be "distracted" by a single faulty data point. This pipeline ensures the clinical integrity of the analysis.

MPL Model:

The AI Ensemble represent two different but advanced levels of AI decision-making.

1. What is 
MLP stands for Multi-Layer Perceptron. This is a type of Artificial Neural Network (ANN).

How it works: It is loosely inspired by the human brain. It consists of multiple layers of "neurons" (mathematical functions) that pass information to one another.
In our code, it is configured with two hidden layers (64 and 32 neurons).
The "Deep Learning" aspect: It is excellent at catching complex, non-linear relationships between biomarkers that simpler models (like Logistic Regression) might miss.

file so it can be reloaded later.
2. What is the AI Ensemble (Majority Vote)?
The AI Ensemble is not a single model, but a "Consensus Committee."

How it works: Instead of trusting just one AI, the system runs your patient data through all trained models simultaneously:
Random Forest (Decision Trees)
Logistic Regression (Statistical Probability)
SVM (Geometric Separation)
XGBoost (Gradient Boosting)
MLP (Neural Network)
The "Majority Vote": If you have 5 models, and 4 say "Detected" while 1 says "Healthy," the Ensemble outputs "Detected".
Clinical Value: This is very similar to a MDT (Multi-Disciplinary Team) meeting in a hospital. One expert might make a mistake, but it is much less likely that 5 different types of "AI experts" will all make the same mistake.

5-Fold Cross-Validation:

5-Fold Cross-Validation is a "stress test" used to prove your AI model is reliable and wasn't just "lucky" with the data.

How it works:
The Split: Our dataset is divided into 5 equal parts (folds).
The Rotation: The training process happens 5 separate times.
In each round, the AI uses 4 parts to learn (Training) and the remaining 1 part as a "blind exam" (Testing).
This rotates until every single patient has been used as part of the "exam" exactly once.
The Average: Instead of one accuracy score, you get 5 different scores. We take the Average of these to determine the model's true performance.
Why it matters for your research:
Proof of Stability: It proves that the AI works well for any patient, not just the ones it saw during training.
Eliminates Bias: It prevents "overfitting" (where the AI basically just memorizes the data).
Clinical Trust: In medicine, 5-Fold Validation is the standard way to prove that an algorithm is robust enough for real-world hospital use.
Summary:

"It is like taking a 5-part exam where the questions keep changing. If the AI passes all 5 parts consistently, we know it truly understands the biological patterns."

Splitting
We split the data using a 80/20 Stratified Split:

Training Set (80%): The "Textbook." The AI uses this large portion to learn the biomarker patterns and correlations.
Test Set (20%): The "Blind Exam." This data is hidden from the AI during training. We use it at the very end to see if the AI can accurately predict "new" patients it has never seen before.
Key Technical Detail: We use "Stratification."
This means we ensure the 80/20 split has the same percentage of "Healthy" vs "Detected" patients in both sets. This prevents the AI from becoming biased toward one group.

The Core Tri-Biomarker Signal
During our extraction pipeline, we specifically isolate and weigh three primary oncological biomarkers out of the 61-feature panel. These form the biological "core" of the AI's diagnostic fingerprint:

1. PSA Concentration (Prostate-Specific Antigen)
   - Clinical Role: The dominant signal in the dataset. Elevated levels (>28,224 pg/mL in our cohort) act as the primary trigger for a "Detected" classification.
   - AI Weight: Accounts for ~86.0% of the ensemble's decision-making mass. The severe skew between healthy (~1,742 pg/mL) and detected cases (~58,205 pg/mL) is exactly why non-linear models like Random Forest excel here.

2. AFP Concentration (Alpha-Fetoprotein)
   - Clinical Role: A protein indicating potential hepatocellular or germ cell involvement. 
   - AI Weight: Contributes ~7.4% to the decision. While its average distribution between healthy and detected cases is similar, extreme outliers act as a secondary "co-signal" to confirm positive diagnoses.

3. CA125 Concentration (Cancer Antigen 125)
   - Clinical Role: Widely used for detecting ovarian/peritoneal pathology.
   - AI Weight: Contributes ~6.6%. Elevated levels topically correlate with the Detected cluster in our t-SNE mapping.

The AI Ensemble does not treat these markers in isolation. It looks for "Co-Biomarker Synergy" — for example, if PSA surges while AFP and CA125 also show abnormal peaks, the clinical confidence of the prediction exponentially increases.
